1. INTRODUCTION TO FINANCIAL STATEMENTS

1.1 Meaning of Financial Statements

Financial statements are formal reports prepared by a business organisation to present its financial performance and financial position for a particular accounting period. They provide information about the income, expenses, assets, liabilities, equity and cash flows of a business.

At the end of an accounting year, every business needs to know whether it has earned a profit or suffered a loss and what its financial position is. Financial statements help provide this information in a systematic manner.

Definition: Financial statements are structured reports that communicate the financial performance and financial position of an organisation to its owners, investors, creditors, management and other interested parties.

1.2 Objectives of Financial Statements

  1. Determining Profit or Loss: To calculate the profit earned or loss suffered during a particular accounting period.
  2. Knowing Financial Position: To determine the assets, liabilities and owner's equity of the business.
  3. Providing Financial Information: To provide useful information to investors, creditors, employees and management.
  4. Decision-Making: To help management make suitable business and investment decisions.
  5. Measuring Performance: To evaluate the profitability and operational efficiency of the business.
  6. Financial Control: To help management monitor expenses and control the use of resources.
  7. Legal Compliance: To support statutory reporting and compliance with applicable accounting and legal requirements.

1.3 Importance of Financial Statements

  • They show the profitability of a business.
  • They provide information about financial stability.
  • They help investors evaluate investment opportunities.
  • They help banks and creditors assess repayment capacity.
  • They support business planning and budgeting.
  • They help compare financial performance over different years.
  • They improve transparency and accountability.

1.4 Main Components of Financial Statements

Financial Statement Meaning
Trading Account Prepared to calculate Gross Profit or Gross Loss from trading activities.
Profit and Loss Account Prepared to calculate Net Profit or Net Loss after considering indirect expenses and incomes.
Balance Sheet Prepared to show the assets, liabilities and owner's equity on a particular date.
Cash Flow Statement Shows cash inflows and outflows from operating, investing and financing activities, where applicable.

2. TRADING ACCOUNT

2.1 Meaning of Trading Account

A Trading Account is an account prepared by a trading business at the end of an accounting period to determine Gross Profit or Gross Loss from buying and selling goods.

It records direct expenses and the cost of goods sold. It helps determine the difference between net sales and the cost of goods sold.

Definition: Trading Account is a nominal account prepared to calculate Gross Profit or Gross Loss by comparing net sales with the cost of goods sold during an accounting period.

2.2 Objectives of Trading Account

  • To calculate Gross Profit or Gross Loss.
  • To determine the cost of goods sold.
  • To know the efficiency of purchasing and selling activities.
  • To provide information for preparing the Profit and Loss Account.
  • To compare trading performance between different accounting periods.

2.3 Items Recorded in Trading Account

A. Opening Stock

Opening stock refers to the value of goods available at the beginning of an accounting year. It is the closing stock of the previous accounting year.

B. Purchases

Purchases represent goods purchased by a business for resale during the accounting period.

Net Purchases are calculated after deducting purchase returns and applicable trade discounts.

Net Purchases = Purchases - Purchase Returns

C. Direct Expenses

Direct expenses are the expenses directly related to purchasing, bringing or producing goods in a saleable condition.

Examples:

  • Carriage Inward
  • Freight Inward
  • Direct Wages
  • Factory Power
  • Import Duty on Purchases
  • Manufacturing Expenses

D. Sales

Sales refer to the total value of goods sold by the business during the accounting period.

Net Sales = Sales - Sales Returns

E. Closing Stock

Closing stock refers to the value of goods remaining unsold at the end of an accounting period.

It is generally valued at cost or net realisable value, whichever is lower, subject to the applicable accounting requirements.

2.4 Cost of Goods Sold

Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) refers to the total cost of goods that have been sold during an accounting period.

COGS = Opening Stock + Net Purchases
+ Direct Expenses - Closing Stock

2.5 Gross Profit and Gross Loss

Gross Profit is the excess of net sales over the cost of goods sold. When the cost of goods sold is greater than net sales, the business suffers a Gross Loss.

Gross Profit = Net Sales - Cost of Goods Sold
Gross Loss = Cost of Goods Sold - Net Sales

2.7 Numerical Example of Trading Account

Problem:

From the following information, calculate Gross Profit:

ParticularsAmount (₹)
Opening Stock20,000
Purchases50,000
Direct Wages10,000
Carriage Inward5,000
Sales1,00,000

3. PROFIT AND LOSS ACCOUNT

3.1 Meaning of Profit and Loss Account

A Profit and Loss Account is prepared after the Trading Account to calculate the Net Profit or Net Loss of a business during an accounting period.

It records indirect expenses, losses, indirect incomes and gains that are not included in the Trading Account.

Definition: Profit and Loss Account is a nominal account prepared to determine the net financial result of a business by comparing indirect expenses and losses with indirect incomes and gains.

3.2 Objectives of Profit and Loss Account

  1. To calculate Net Profit or Net Loss.
  2. To record indirect expenses and incomes.
  3. To determine the overall profitability of a business.
  4. To help management control operating expenses.
  5. To provide information for business decisions.

3.3 Items Recorded in Profit and Loss Account

A. Indirect Expenses

Indirect expenses are expenses that are not directly related to the purchase or production of goods but are necessary for running the business.

Examples:

  • Office Salaries
  • Office Rent
  • Advertising Expenses
  • Insurance Expenses
  • Depreciation
  • Printing and Stationery
  • Telephone Expenses
  • Interest on Loan
  • Bad Debts

B. Indirect Incomes

Indirect incomes are incomes earned from activities other than the main trading operations of the business.

Examples:

  • Commission Received
  • Interest Received
  • Rent Received
  • Discount Received
  • Profit on Sale of Fixed Assets

3.4 Net Profit and Net Loss

Net Profit is the excess of total income over total expenses of a business during a particular accounting period.

Net Profit = Gross Profit + Other Incomes
- Indirect Expenses

If the total expenses exceed the total income, the business suffers a Net Loss.

Net Loss = Total Expenses - Total Income

3.5 Format of Profit and Loss Account

PROFIT AND LOSS ACCOUNT

Dr. Particulars Amount (₹) Cr. Particulars Amount (₹)
To Salaries 8,000 By Gross Profit b/d 30,000
To Rent 4,000 By Commission Received 2,000
To Advertising 3,000
To Depreciation 2,000

Total 32,000 Total 32,000

3.6 Numerical Example of Net Profit

Problem:

A business has Gross Profit of ₹50,000. Its indirect expenses are salaries ₹10,000, rent ₹5,000 and advertising ₹3,000. Commission received is ₹2,000. Calculate Net Profit.

Solution:
Gross Profit = ₹50,000
Add: Commission Received = ₹2,000
Total Income = ₹52,000
Less: Salaries = ₹10,000
Less: Rent = ₹5,000
Less: Advertising = ₹3,000
Total Expenses = ₹18,000

Net Profit = ₹52,000 - ₹18,000
Net Profit = ₹34,000.

4. BALANCE SHEET

4.1 Meaning of Balance Sheet

A Balance Sheet is a financial statement prepared on a particular date to show the financial position of a business. It presents the assets, liabilities and owner's equity of an organisation.

It helps determine what the business owns and what it owes to outsiders.

Definition: A Balance Sheet is a statement showing the assets, liabilities and owner's equity of a business on a particular date.

4.2 Objectives of Balance Sheet

  • To show the financial position of a business.
  • To determine the total assets and liabilities.
  • To calculate owner's equity.
  • To assess the short-term and long-term financial position.
  • To provide information to investors and creditors.
  • To support financial planning and decision-making.

4.3 Classification of Assets

A. Non-Current Assets

Non-current assets are assets held for long-term use in the business and are not normally intended for sale in the ordinary course of business.

Examples:

  • Land and Building
  • Plant and Machinery
  • Furniture
  • Vehicles
  • Long-term Investments
  • Goodwill
  • Patents

B. Current Assets

Current assets are assets expected to be realised, sold or consumed during the normal operating cycle or within twelve months, subject to the applicable accounting framework.

Examples:

  • Cash in Hand
  • Cash at Bank
  • Debtors
  • Closing Stock
  • Bills Receivable
  • Prepaid Expenses
  • Short-term Investments

4.4 Classification of Liabilities

A. Non-Current Liabilities

Non-current liabilities are financial obligations that are generally payable after twelve months.

Examples: Long-term bank loans, debentures and long-term borrowings.

B. Current Liabilities

Current liabilities are obligations expected to be settled during the normal operating cycle or within twelve months, subject to applicable accounting requirements.

Examples:

  • Creditors
  • Bills Payable
  • Outstanding Expenses
  • Short-term Loans
  • Income Received in Advance

4.6 Accounting Equation and Balance Sheet

Assets = Liabilities + Owner's Equity

The Balance Sheet is based on the accounting equation. The total value of assets must always be equal to the combined value of liabilities and owner's equity.

5. MEANING AND IMPORTANCE OF ADJUSTMENTS

5.1 Meaning of Adjustments

Adjustments are accounting entries passed at the end of an accounting period to ensure that all incomes and expenses are recorded in the correct period and that assets and liabilities are shown at appropriate amounts.

Some expenses may remain unpaid, some expenses may have been paid in advance, and some income may have been earned but not received. Such items must be adjusted before preparing the final financial statements.

Definition: Adjustments are entries made at the end of an accounting period to record outstanding, prepaid, accrued and other items so that financial statements present a fair and accurate picture of the business.

5.2 Objectives of Adjustments

  1. To follow the accrual basis of accounting.
  2. To record income in the period in which it is earned.
  3. To record expenses in the period in which they are incurred.
  4. To determine the correct profit or loss.
  5. To show assets and liabilities at appropriate amounts.
  6. To ensure proper presentation of financial statements.

5.3 Importance of Adjustments

  • Helps calculate accurate net profit or loss.
  • Ensures correct valuation of assets and liabilities.
  • Follows the matching principle.
  • Prevents overstatement or understatement of income and expenses.
  • Provides reliable financial information.
  • Improves the accuracy of financial statements.

6. TYPES OF ADJUSTMENTS IN FINANCIAL STATEMENTS

6.1 Outstanding Expenses

Outstanding expenses are expenses that have been incurred during an accounting period but have not yet been paid by the business.

These expenses belong to the current accounting period and must be charged to the Profit and Loss Account even if payment is made later.

Examples: Outstanding salary, outstanding rent, outstanding wages and outstanding electricity charges.

Example:

Salary of ₹5,000 is due but has not been paid at the end of the accounting year.

Adjustment Entry:
Salary A/c Dr. ₹5,000
    To Outstanding Salary A/c ₹5,000

Treatment:
  • Added to salary in the Profit and Loss Account.
  • Shown as a current liability in the Balance Sheet.

6.2 Prepaid Expenses

Prepaid expenses are expenses that have been paid during the current accounting period but relate partly or wholly to a future accounting period.

Such expenses are deducted from the current year's expense because the future benefit has not yet been consumed.

Examples: Prepaid insurance, prepaid rent and prepaid subscription.

Example:

Insurance paid during the year is ₹12,000, out of which ₹2,000 relates to the next accounting year.

Adjustment Entry:
Prepaid Insurance A/c Dr. ₹2,000
    To Insurance A/c ₹2,000

Treatment:
  • Deduct ₹2,000 from insurance expense in the Profit and Loss Account.
  • Show ₹2,000 as a current asset in the Balance Sheet.

6.3 Accrued Income

Accrued income is income that has been earned during the accounting period but has not yet been received in cash.

Examples: Interest accrued, commission receivable and rent receivable.

Example:

Interest of ₹3,000 has been earned but not yet received.

Adjustment Entry:
Accrued Interest A/c Dr. ₹3,000
    To Interest Received A/c ₹3,000

Treatment:
  • Add ₹3,000 to interest income in the Profit and Loss Account.
  • Show ₹3,000 as a current asset in the Balance Sheet.

6.4 Income Received in Advance

Income received in advance refers to income received during the current accounting period for services or benefits that will be provided in a future accounting period.

Examples: Rent received in advance, commission received in advance and subscription received in advance.

Example:

Rent received during the year is ₹15,000, out of which ₹3,000 relates to the next accounting year.

Adjustment Entry:
Rent Received A/c Dr. ₹3,000
    To Rent Received in Advance A/c ₹3,000

Treatment:
  • Deduct ₹3,000 from rent income in the Profit and Loss Account.
  • Show ₹3,000 as a current liability in the Balance Sheet.

6.5 Depreciation

Depreciation is the systematic allocation of the depreciable amount of a tangible fixed asset over its useful life. It arises due to usage, passage of time, wear and tear and obsolescence.

Examples: Depreciation on machinery, furniture, vehicles and buildings.

Objectives of Charging Depreciation

  • To calculate the correct profit of a business.
  • To show fixed assets at appropriate carrying amounts.
  • To recognise the cost of using an asset over its useful life.
  • To follow the matching principle.
  • To provide a systematic method of allocating asset cost.

Methods of Depreciation

A. Straight Line Method (SLM)

Under this method, an equal amount of depreciation is charged every year throughout the useful life of an asset, assuming no change in the relevant estimates.

Annual Depreciation = (Cost - Residual Value)
÷ Useful Life
Example:

Machinery costs ₹1,00,000. Its estimated residual value is ₹10,000 and useful life is 9 years.

Annual Depreciation = (1,00,000 - 10,000) ÷ 9
= ₹90,000 ÷ 9
Annual Depreciation = ₹10,000.

B. Written Down Value Method (WDV)

Under this method, depreciation is charged at a fixed percentage on the opening carrying amount of the asset each year. Therefore, the amount of depreciation generally decreases every year.

Depreciation = Opening Carrying Amount
× Depreciation Rate
Example:

Machinery costs ₹1,00,000 and depreciation is charged at 10% per annum under the WDV method.

YearOpening Value (₹)Depreciation (₹)Closing Value (₹)
11,00,00010,00090,000
290,0009,00081,000
381,0008,10072,900

Accounting Treatment of Depreciation

Journal Entry:

Depreciation A/c Dr.
    To Asset A/c

Treatment:

  • Depreciation is debited to the Profit and Loss Account.
  • The asset's carrying amount is reduced in the Balance Sheet.

6.6 Bad Debts

Bad debts are amounts due from customers that have become irrecoverable and are written off as a loss by the business.

They generally arise when customers become unable or unwilling to pay the amount owed.

Example:

A debtor named Ram owes ₹5,000, but the amount is found to be irrecoverable.

Journal Entry:
Bad Debts A/c Dr. ₹5,000
    To Ram A/c ₹5,000

Treatment:
  • Bad debts are charged to the Profit and Loss Account.
  • Debtors are reduced in the Balance Sheet.

6.7 Provision for Doubtful Debts

Provision for doubtful debts is an estimate of the amount that may not be recovered from debtors. It is created to recognise the expected credit losses on receivables.

It helps ensure that debtors are not shown at an amount greater than the amount expected to be collected.

Example:

Total debtors are ₹50,000 and the business estimates doubtful debts at 5%.

Provision = ₹50,000 × 5%
Provision = ₹2,500.

Accounting Treatment:

  • The required provision is charged as an expense in the Profit and Loss Account, after considering any existing provision.
  • The provision is deducted from debtors in the Balance Sheet.

6.8 Interest on Capital

Interest on capital refers to the amount of interest allowed to the owner on the capital invested in the business, when such an allowance is provided for under the agreement or applicable arrangement.

Interest on Capital = Capital × Rate of Interest
× Time / 100
Example:

Capital is ₹1,00,000 and interest is allowed at 10% per annum for a full year.

Interest = 1,00,000 × 10 / 100
Interest on Capital = ₹10,000.

Treatment in Sole Proprietorship Final Accounts:

  • Debited to the Profit and Loss Account.
  • Added to the owner's capital in the Balance Sheet.

6.9 Interest on Drawings

Interest on drawings is the amount charged to the owner for withdrawing cash or goods from the business for personal use, when such interest is provided for in the arrangement.

Interest on Drawings = Drawings × Rate × Time / 100

Treatment:

  • Credited to the Profit and Loss Account.
  • Deducted from the owner's capital in the Balance Sheet.

6.10 Closing Stock

Closing stock is the value of unsold goods remaining at the end of the accounting period. It represents goods that are available for sale in the next accounting period.

Closing stock is generally valued at cost or net realisable value, whichever is lower, in accordance with the applicable accounting requirements.

Adjustment Entry:

Closing Stock A/c Dr.
    To Trading A/c

Treatment:

  • Shown on the credit side of the Trading Account.
  • Shown as a current asset in the Balance Sheet.

6.11 Goods Distributed as Free Samples

Sometimes a business distributes goods without charging customers for advertising and promotional purposes. Such goods are treated as advertising expenses.

Adjustment Entry:

Advertisement A/c Dr.
    To Purchases A/c

Treatment:

  • Deducted from purchases in the Trading Account.
  • Added to advertising expenses in the Profit and Loss Account.

6.12 Goods Withdrawn by Owner for Personal Use

When the owner withdraws goods from the business for personal use, such goods are treated as drawings.

Journal Entry:

Drawings A/c Dr.
    To Purchases A/c

Treatment:

  • Deducted from purchases in the Trading Account.
  • Deducted from capital in the Balance Sheet.

7. FINANCIAL STATEMENTS WITH ADJUSTMENTS

7.1 Meaning of Financial Statements with Adjustments

Financial statements with adjustments are final accounts prepared after considering all necessary adjustments relating to the accounting period.

Adjustments are made to ensure that the financial statements show the correct profit or loss and the appropriate financial position of the business.

A business must consider outstanding expenses, prepaid expenses, accrued income, income received in advance, depreciation, bad debts and other relevant items before preparing final accounts.

7.2 Why Are Adjustments Necessary?

  1. To calculate the correct profit or loss.
  2. To follow the accrual basis of accounting.
  3. To apply the matching principle.
  4. To show assets at appropriate values.
  5. To show all liabilities correctly.
  6. To avoid overstatement or understatement of income and expenses.

7.3 Important Rule: Double Effect of Adjustments

In traditional final accounts problems, most adjustments have two effects. One effect is recorded in the relevant Trading Account or Profit and Loss Account, while the other is recorded in the Balance Sheet.

Remember: If an adjustment is given outside the Trial Balance, it is generally shown at two places in final accounts. If an adjusted item is already included in the Trial Balance, its treatment is generally shown only at the appropriate place because its corresponding entry has already been recorded.

7.4 Illustration of Double Effect

Adjustment First Effect Second Effect
Outstanding Salary Add to salary in P&L. Show as current liability.
Prepaid Insurance Deduct from insurance expense. Show as current asset.
Accrued Commission Add to commission income. Show as current asset.
Rent Received in Advance Deduct from rent income. Show as current liability.
Depreciation Debit to P&L. Deduct from asset value.
Closing Stock Credit to Trading Account. Show as current asset.

7.5 Steps for Preparing Financial Statements with Adjustments

  1. Step 1: Prepare the Trial Balance from the Ledger Accounts.
  2. Step 2: Read all additional adjustment information carefully.
  3. Step 3: Identify the two effects of each adjustment.
  4. Step 4: Prepare the Trading Account and calculate Gross Profit or Gross Loss.
  5. Step 5: Prepare the Profit and Loss Account and calculate Net Profit or Net Loss.
  6. Step 6: Adjust capital for additional capital, profit or loss and drawings.
  7. Step 7: Prepare the Balance Sheet after considering all assets and liabilities.
  8. Step 8: Verify that total assets equal total liabilities plus owner's equity.

9. IMPORTANT FORMULAS FOR EXAMINATION

Net Sales = Sales - Sales Returns
Net Purchases = Purchases - Purchase Returns
Cost of Goods Sold = Opening Stock + Net Purchases
+ Direct Expenses - Closing Stock
Gross Profit = Net Sales - Cost of Goods Sold
Net Profit = Gross Profit + Other Incomes
- Indirect Expenses
Assets = Liabilities + Owner's Equity
Depreciation (SLM) = (Cost - Residual Value)
÷ Useful Life
Depreciation (WDV) = Opening Carrying Amount
× Depreciation Rate
Provision for Doubtful Debts = Debtors
× Provision Rate

11. CONCLUSION

Financial statement preparation is an important part of financial accounting. Trading Account helps calculate Gross Profit or Gross Loss, Profit and Loss Account determines Net Profit or Net Loss, and the Balance Sheet shows the financial position of a business on a particular date.

Adjustments are equally important because they ensure that all incomes and expenses are recorded in the correct accounting period. Items such as outstanding expenses, prepaid expenses, accrued income, depreciation, bad debts and closing stock must be properly adjusted before preparing final accounts.

A proper understanding of financial statements and adjustments helps students develop practical accounting knowledge and prepares them to solve numerical problems related to final accounts.
