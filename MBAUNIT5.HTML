1. INTRODUCTION TO FINANCIAL STATEMENT ANALYSIS

1.1 Meaning of Financial Statement Analysis

Financial Statement Analysis refers to the process of examining and interpreting the financial statements of a business to understand its profitability, liquidity, solvency, efficiency and overall financial performance.

Financial statements contain information about sales, expenses, assets, liabilities, capital and cash flows. However, this information becomes more useful when it is properly analysed and compared with previous years, competitors and industry standards.

Definition: Financial Statement Analysis is the systematic examination and interpretation of financial information to evaluate the financial performance and financial position of a business organisation.

1.2 Objectives of Financial Statement Analysis

  1. Measuring Profitability: To determine whether a business is earning sufficient profit from its operations.
  2. Evaluating Liquidity: To examine the ability of a business to pay its short-term liabilities.
  3. Measuring Solvency: To determine the ability of a business to meet its long-term financial obligations.
  4. Evaluating Efficiency: To measure how effectively the business uses its assets and resources.
  5. Comparing Performance: To compare financial performance across different accounting periods.
  6. Supporting Decisions: To help investors, creditors and management make informed decisions.
  7. Identifying Weaknesses: To identify financial problems and areas requiring improvement.
  8. Future Planning: To help management prepare budgets, forecasts and financial strategies.

1.3 Importance of Financial Statement Analysis

  • Helps determine the profitability of a business.
  • Shows the financial strength and weaknesses of an organisation.
  • Helps investors evaluate investment opportunities.
  • Assists banks in evaluating loan repayment capacity.
  • Helps management control costs and expenses.
  • Provides information for future planning.
  • Helps compare the performance of different businesses.
  • Supports better financial decision-making.

1.4 Users of Financial Statement Analysis

Users Purpose of Analysis
Management To evaluate business performance, control costs and make decisions.
Investors To assess profitability, risk and future returns on investment.
Creditors To evaluate the ability of a business to pay its debts.
Banks and Financial Institutions To assess creditworthiness before granting loans.
Employees To understand the financial stability and growth prospects of the organisation.
Government To examine financial information for taxation and regulatory purposes.
Customers To assess the long-term stability of important suppliers.

1.5 Limitations of Financial Statement Analysis

  1. It is based mainly on historical financial information.
  2. It may be affected by differences in accounting policies.
  3. Inflation can reduce the usefulness of comparisons over time.
  4. Financial statements may not show all non-financial factors.
  5. Different businesses may follow different accounting methods.
  6. Window dressing may affect the presentation of financial information.
  7. Analysis depends on the accuracy and reliability of the original data.

2. TOOLS AND TECHNIQUES OF FINANCIAL STATEMENT ANALYSIS

2.1 Meaning of Tools of Analysis

Tools of Financial Statement Analysis are the methods and techniques used to examine financial information and understand the performance and position of a business.

These tools help convert accounting figures into meaningful information that can be used for comparison, evaluation and decision-making.

2.2 Major Tools of Financial Statement Analysis

Tool Meaning Main Purpose
Comparative Statements Comparison of financial statements of two or more periods. To identify changes in financial performance.
Common-Size Statements Presentation of financial items as percentages of a common base. To understand the composition of financial statements.
Trend Analysis Study of financial changes over several years. To identify long-term trends.
Ratio Analysis Study of relationships between financial figures. To measure liquidity, profitability and solvency.
Cash Flow Analysis Analysis of cash inflows and cash outflows. To understand cash generation and utilisation.

2.3 Classification of Analytical Techniques

A. Horizontal Analysis

It compares financial figures of different accounting periods to identify increases and decreases.

B. Vertical Analysis

It expresses each financial item as a percentage of a common base figure in the same financial statement.

C. Trend Analysis

It examines financial data over several years to identify the direction and pattern of changes.

D. Ratio Analysis

It establishes relationships between different financial figures to evaluate the financial health of a business.

E. Cash Flow Analysis

It studies the sources and uses of cash during a particular accounting period.

2.4 Advantages of Financial Analysis Tools

  • Make financial information easier to understand.
  • Help compare financial results.
  • Identify business strengths and weaknesses.
  • Support investment and lending decisions.
  • Help in financial planning and forecasting.
  • Improve management control.

3. HORIZONTAL ANALYSIS

3.1 Meaning of Horizontal Analysis

Horizontal Analysis is a method of financial statement analysis in which financial figures of two or more accounting periods are compared to determine the changes in amount and percentage.

It is also known as Comparative Financial Statement Analysis because it compares similar financial items across different years.

Definition: Horizontal Analysis is the technique of comparing financial data over different accounting periods to identify increases, decreases and percentage changes in financial items.

3.2 Objectives of Horizontal Analysis

  • To compare financial performance between different years.
  • To identify increases and decreases in revenue and expenses.
  • To examine changes in assets and liabilities.
  • To evaluate the growth of a business.
  • To identify unusual financial changes.
  • To support future financial planning.

3.3 Formula of Horizontal Analysis

Absolute Change = Current Year Amount
- Previous Year Amount
Percentage Change = (Absolute Change
÷ Previous Year Amount) × 100

3.4 Numerical Example of Horizontal Analysis

Problem:

The following information is available for ABC Ltd. for two years. Prepare a comparative statement using Horizontal Analysis.

Particulars 2024 (₹) 2025 (₹)
Sales5,00,0006,00,000
Cost of Goods Sold3,00,0003,60,000
Operating Expenses1,00,0001,20,000
Net Profit1,00,0001,20,000
Solution:
Particulars 2024 (₹) 2025 (₹) Change (₹) Change (%)
Sales5,00,0006,00,0001,00,00020%
Cost of Goods Sold3,00,0003,60,00060,00020%
Operating Expenses1,00,0001,20,00020,00020%
Net Profit1,00,0001,20,00020,00020%
Calculation of Sales Percentage Change:
= (1,00,000 ÷ 5,00,000) × 100
= 20%

Interpretation:

Sales increased by 20% during 2025 compared with 2024. Cost of goods sold and operating expenses also increased by 20%. Net profit increased by 20%, indicating that profit grew at the same rate as sales.

3.5 Advantages of Horizontal Analysis

  • Helps compare financial performance across years.
  • Shows the growth or decline of business activities.
  • Helps identify unusual changes.
  • Useful for future planning and forecasting.
  • Easy to understand and calculate.

3.6 Limitations of Horizontal Analysis

  • It mainly focuses on historical data.
  • Inflation may affect comparisons.
  • Changes in accounting policies may reduce comparability.
  • It does not explain the reasons behind every change.
  • It is less useful when the base period is abnormal.

4. VERTICAL ANALYSIS

4.1 Meaning of Vertical Analysis

Vertical Analysis is a technique of financial statement analysis in which each item of a financial statement is expressed as a percentage of a common base figure for the same accounting period.

It is also known as Common-Size Analysis. It helps understand the relative importance of different financial items.

Definition: Vertical Analysis is the method of expressing individual financial statement items as percentages of a common base figure to study the composition of financial statements.

4.2 Objectives of Vertical Analysis

  • To understand the composition of financial statements.
  • To determine the proportion of expenses to sales.
  • To examine the relationship between assets and total assets.
  • To compare businesses of different sizes.
  • To identify changes in the cost and expense structure.
  • To evaluate financial efficiency.

4.3 Common Base Figures

Financial Statement Common Base Figure
Income Statement Net Sales or Revenue from Operations.
Balance Sheet Total Assets or Total Equity and Liabilities.

4.4 Formula of Vertical Analysis

Common-Size Percentage =
(Individual Item ÷ Common Base) × 100

4.5 Numerical Example of Vertical Analysis

Problem:

Calculate the common-size percentages from the following information:

Sales5,00,000
Cost of Goods Sold3,00,000
Operating Expenses1,00,000
Net Profit1,00,000
Solution:

Sales are taken as the common base figure, which is equal to 100%.

ParticularsAmount (₹)Percentage

Cost of Goods Sold3,00,00060%
Operating Expenses1,00,00020%
Net Profit1,00,00020%
Calculation:
Cost of Goods Sold = (3,00,000 ÷ 5,00,000) × 100
= 60%

Interpretation:

Cost of goods sold represents 60% of sales, operating expenses represent 20% and net profit represents 20%. Thus, the business earns a net profit of ₹20 for every ₹100 of sales.

4.7 Advantages of Vertical Analysis

  • Helps understand the structure of financial statements.
  • Useful for comparing businesses of different sizes.
  • Shows the proportion of expenses to sales.
  • Helps identify unnecessary expenditure.
  • Provides useful information for management decisions.

4.8 Limitations of Vertical Analysis

  • It may not show changes over several years.
  • It depends on the accuracy of accounting information.
  • It does not explain the causes of financial changes.
  • Different accounting policies may affect comparisons.
  • It does not provide a complete picture of financial performance by itself.

4.9 Difference Between Horizontal and Vertical Analysis

5. TREND ANALYSIS

5.1 Meaning of Trend Analysis

Trend Analysis is a technique of financial statement analysis in which financial figures of several accounting periods are examined to identify the direction and pattern of changes over time.

It helps understand whether sales, profits, expenses, assets and liabilities are increasing, decreasing or remaining relatively stable over several years.

Definition: Trend Analysis is the method of studying financial data over a number of years by taking one year as the base year and expressing the figures of other years as percentages of the base year.

5.2 Objectives of Trend Analysis

  • To identify long-term financial growth.
  • To study changes in sales and profits.
  • To examine the movement of expenses.
  • To identify favourable and unfavourable trends.
  • To help forecast future financial performance.
  • To support long-term planning and investment decisions.

5.3 Formula of Trend Analysis

Trend Percentage = (Current Year Figure
÷ Base Year Figure) × 100
Important: The base year is normally assigned a value of 100%. All other years are compared with this base year.

5.4 Numerical Example of Trend Analysis

20243,00,000
20253,60,000
Solution:
YearSales (₹)Trend Percentage
20222,00,000100%
20232,40,000120%
20243,00,000150%
20253,60,000180%
Calculation for 2023:
= (2,40,000 ÷ 2,00,000) × 100
= 120%

Interpretation:

Sales increased from ₹2,00,000 in 2022 to ₹3,60,000 in 2025. The trend percentage of 180% indicates that sales in 2025 were 80% higher than sales in the base year.

5.5 Advantages of Trend Analysis

  • Shows the long-term direction of business performance.
  • Helps identify growth patterns.
  • Useful for forecasting future sales and profits.
  • Helps management prepare budgets.
  • Provides information about financial stability.

5.6 Limitations of Trend Analysis

  • It depends on the selection of a suitable base year.
  • Inflation may affect financial comparisons.
  • Past trends may not continue in the future.
  • Changes in accounting methods may affect results.
  • Abnormal events may distort the trend.

6. RATIO ANALYSIS

6.1 Meaning of Ratio Analysis

Ratio Analysis is a technique of financial statement analysis in which relationships between two or more financial figures are calculated and interpreted to evaluate the financial health of a business.

Ratios help measure liquidity, profitability, solvency and operational efficiency. They are useful to management, investors, banks and creditors.

Definition: Ratio Analysis is the process of calculating and interpreting relationships between financial statement items to assess the financial performance and position of an organisation.

6.2 Objectives of Ratio Analysis

  1. To measure short-term financial strength.
  2. To evaluate long-term solvency.
  3. To determine business profitability.
  4. To measure operational efficiency.
  5. To compare financial performance over different years.
  6. To support investment and credit decisions.
  7. To identify financial weaknesses.

6.3 Classification of Accounting Ratios

Accounting ratios are generally divided into four major categories:

1. Liquidity Ratios
2. Solvency Ratios
3. Activity or Turnover Ratios
4. Profitability Ratios

6.4 Liquidity Ratios

Liquidity ratios measure the ability of a business to meet its short-term financial obligations as and when they become due.

A. Current Ratio

The Current Ratio measures the relationship between current assets and current liabilities. It indicates the ability of a business to meet its short-term obligations using current assets.

Current Ratio = Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities
Example:
Current Assets = ₹2,00,000
Current Liabilities = ₹1,00,000
Current Ratio = 2,00,000 ÷ 1,00,000
Current Ratio = 2 : 1.

A ratio of 2:1 is often used as a traditional reference point, but the suitable ratio depends on the industry and nature of business.

B. Quick Ratio

Quick Ratio measures the ability of a business to meet its short-term liabilities using highly liquid current assets.

Quick Ratio = Quick Assets ÷ Current Liabilities

Quick assets generally include current assets excluding inventory and prepaid expenses.

Example:
Current Assets = ₹2,00,000
Inventory = ₹50,000
Prepaid Expenses = ₹10,000
Current Liabilities = ₹1,00,000
Quick Assets = 2,00,000 - 50,000 - 10,000
= ₹1,40,000
Quick Ratio = 1,40,000 ÷ 1,00,000
Quick Ratio = 1.4 : 1.

6.5 Solvency Ratios

Solvency ratios measure the ability of a business to meet its long-term financial obligations. They also help evaluate the capital structure and financial risk of an organisation.

A. Debt-Equity Ratio

This ratio measures the relationship between long-term debt and shareholders' funds or owners' equity, depending on the prescribed definition used.

Debt-Equity Ratio = Long-Term Debt
÷ Shareholders' Funds
Example:
Long-Term Debt = ₹3,00,000
Shareholders' Funds = ₹6,00,000
Debt-Equity Ratio = 3,00,000 ÷ 6,00,000
Debt-Equity Ratio = 0.5 : 1.

B. Proprietary Ratio

The Proprietary Ratio measures the proportion of total assets financed by owners' funds.

Proprietary Ratio = Shareholders' Funds
÷ Total Assets × 100

C. Interest Coverage Ratio

This ratio measures the ability of a business to pay interest on its borrowings from its operating earnings.

Interest Coverage Ratio = EBIT
÷ Interest Expense

EBIT means Earnings Before Interest and Tax.

6.6 Activity or Turnover Ratios

Activity ratios measure how efficiently a business uses its assets and resources to generate sales and revenue.

A. Inventory Turnover Ratio

This ratio measures how many times inventory is sold or used during an accounting period.

Inventory Turnover Ratio = Cost of Goods Sold
÷ Average Inventory
Example:
Cost of Goods Sold = ₹4,00,000
Opening Inventory = ₹50,000
Closing Inventory = ₹50,000
Average Inventory = (50,000 + 50,000) ÷ 2
= ₹50,000
Inventory Turnover Ratio = 4,00,000 ÷ 50,000
Inventory Turnover Ratio = 8 Times.

B. Debtors Turnover Ratio

This ratio measures how efficiently a business collects money from its credit customers.

Debtors Turnover Ratio = Net Credit Sales
÷ Average Trade Receivables

C. Average Collection Period

It shows the average number of days taken by a business to collect money from its credit customers.

Average Collection Period = 365 Days
÷ Debtors Turnover Ratio

D. Creditors Turnover Ratio

This ratio measures how efficiently a business makes payments to its credit suppliers.

Creditors Turnover Ratio = Net Credit Purchases
÷ Average Trade Payables

E. Working Capital Turnover Ratio

This ratio measures how efficiently the business uses its working capital to generate revenue.

Working Capital Turnover Ratio = Net Sales
÷ Average Working Capital

Working Capital = Current Assets - Current Liabilities.

6.7 Profitability Ratios

Profitability ratios measure the ability of a business to generate profits from sales, assets and owners' investment.

A. Gross Profit Ratio

This ratio measures the percentage of gross profit earned on net sales.

Gross Profit Ratio = (Gross Profit ÷ Net Sales)
× 100
Example:
Gross Profit = ₹2,00,000
Net Sales = ₹5,00,000
Gross Profit Ratio = (2,00,000 ÷ 5,00,000) × 100
Gross Profit Ratio = 40%.

B. Net Profit Ratio

The Net Profit Ratio shows the percentage of net profit earned on net sales. The exact profit measure should be clearly defined when calculating this ratio.

Net Profit Ratio = (Net Profit ÷ Net Sales)
× 100

C. Operating Ratio

This ratio shows the proportion of operating costs in relation to net sales. A lower ratio may indicate better operating efficiency, depending on the business.

Operating Ratio = (Cost of Goods Sold
+ Operating Expenses) ÷ Net Sales × 100

D. Return on Capital Employed (ROCE)

ROCE measures the return generated from the long-term capital invested in the business.

ROCE = (EBIT ÷ Average Capital Employed) × 100

E. Return on Equity (ROE)

This ratio measures the return earned on shareholders' equity.

ROE = (Profit After Tax ÷ Average Shareholders'
Equity) × 100

6.9 Advantages of Ratio Analysis

  • Helps measure profitability and efficiency.
  • Helps evaluate short-term liquidity.
  • Measures long-term financial stability.
  • Supports comparison between different businesses.
  • Helps management identify financial weaknesses.
  • Useful for investors and creditors.

6.10 Limitations of Ratio Analysis

  • Ratios are based on historical financial data.
  • Different accounting policies may affect comparison.
  • Inflation can distort financial figures.
  • One ratio alone cannot provide a complete picture.
  • Window dressing may influence ratios.
  • Industry differences make direct comparison difficult.
  • Qualitative factors are not fully reflected in ratios.

7. CASH FLOW ANALYSIS

7.1 Meaning of Cash Flow Analysis

Cash Flow Analysis is the process of examining the movement of cash and cash equivalents into and out of a business during a particular accounting period.

It helps determine where the business receives cash from and how that cash is utilised. It also helps evaluate the ability of a business to generate sufficient cash to pay its expenses, repay loans and make investments.

Definition: Cash Flow Analysis is the study of cash inflows and cash outflows of a business to evaluate its cash-generating ability and cash management.

7.2 Meaning of Cash and Cash Equivalents

A. Cash

Cash includes cash in hand and demand deposits with banks.

B. Cash Equivalents

Cash equivalents are short-term, highly liquid investments that can readily be converted into known amounts of cash and are subject to an insignificant risk of changes in value. They are generally held to meet short-term cash commitments rather than for investment purposes.

An investment normally qualifies as a cash equivalent only when it has a short maturity, generally three months or less from the date of acquisition.

Examples: Cash in hand, demand deposits and qualifying short-term highly liquid investments.

7.3 Objectives of Cash Flow Analysis

  • To identify the sources of cash received.
  • To determine the uses of cash.
  • To evaluate the liquidity position of a business.
  • To assess the ability to repay loans and other obligations.
  • To examine cash generated from operating activities.
  • To support investment and financing decisions.
  • To assist in cash budgeting and financial planning.

7.4 Classification of Cash Flows

According to AS-3 (Revised), cash flows are classified into three major activities:

A. Operating Activities

Operating activities are the principal revenue-producing activities of a business and other activities that are not investing or financing activities.

Examples:

  • Cash received from customers.
  • Cash paid to suppliers.
  • Cash paid to employees.
  • Cash received from service activities.
  • Cash paid for operating expenses.
  • Income tax payments, unless specifically identified with investing or financing activities.

B. Investing Activities

Investing activities involve the acquisition and disposal of long-term assets and other investments that are not included in cash equivalents.

Examples:

  • Purchase of land and buildings.
  • Purchase of machinery.
  • Sale of fixed assets.
  • Purchase of long-term investments.
  • Sale of investments.
  • Loans and advances given to other parties, subject to the applicable classification rules.

C. Financing Activities

Financing activities are activities that result in changes in the size and composition of the owner's capital and borrowings of the business.

Examples:

  • Issue of shares.
  • Issue of debentures.
  • Taking bank loans.
  • Repayment of borrowings.
  • Repayment of amounts borrowed.
  • Payment of dividends, where classified as financing activities.

7.5 Difference Between Operating, Investing and Financing Activities

Basis Operating Investing Financing
Meaning Activities related to main business operations. Activities related to long-term assets and investments. Activities related to capital and borrowings.
Purpose To generate revenue. To acquire or dispose of investments and long-term assets. To raise or repay finance.
Examples Cash from customers and payments to suppliers. Purchase and sale of machinery. Issue of shares and repayment of loans.

7.6 Methods of Preparing Cash Flow from Operating Activities

Under AS-3 (Revised), cash flows from operating activities may be reported using either the Direct Method or the Indirect Method.

A. Direct Method

Under the Direct Method, major classes of gross cash receipts and gross cash payments are disclosed separately.

Examples of cash receipts:

  • Cash received from customers.
  • Cash received from commissions.
  • Cash received from other operating activities.

Examples of cash payments:

  • Cash paid to suppliers.
  • Cash paid to employees.
  • Cash paid for operating expenses.
  • Income taxes paid, unless specifically identified with investing or financing activities.

Format of Direct Method

ParticularsAmount (₹)
Cash Received from Customers5,00,000

Under the Indirect Method, net profit or loss is adjusted for:

  • Non-cash expenses such as depreciation.
  • Non-operating items such as profit or loss on sale of fixed assets.
  • Changes in inventory, trade receivables and trade payables.
  • Other operating adjustments.

+ Non-Cash Expenses
- Non-Operating Income

7.8 Advantages of Cash Flow Analysis

  • Helps evaluate liquidity and cash availability.
  • Shows sources and uses of cash.
  • Helps assess repayment capacity.
  • Supports cash budgeting.
  • Helps identify cash shortages.
  • Useful for investors, creditors and management.

7.9 Limitations of Cash Flow Analysis

  • It does not directly measure profitability.
  • It does not show all non-cash transactions.
  • It may be affected by the timing of receipts and payments.
  • It does not provide a complete picture of financial performance alone.
  • It is based on historical cash transactions.

8. ACCOUNTING STANDARD AS-3 (REVISED)

8.1 Meaning of AS-3 (Revised)

AS-3 (Revised) is the Indian Accounting Standard dealing with the preparation and presentation of Cash Flow Statements. It explains how cash and cash equivalent movements during an accounting period should be classified and reported.

The standard helps users understand the cash-generating ability of an organisation and how the organisation uses its cash.

Definition: AS-3 (Revised) is an accounting standard that prescribes the requirements for presenting information about historical changes in cash and cash equivalents through a Cash Flow Statement.

8.2 Objectives of AS-3 (Revised)

  1. To provide information about cash receipts and cash payments.
  2. To explain changes in cash and cash equivalents.
  3. To classify cash flows into operating, investing and financing activities.
  4. To help users evaluate the ability of a business to generate cash.
  5. To help assess the liquidity and financial flexibility of an organisation.
  6. To improve comparability between different organisations.

8.3 Scope of AS-3 (Revised)

AS-3 deals with the preparation and presentation of a Cash Flow Statement for an accounting period.

The standard applies to the preparation of cash flow information by enterprises required to prepare such statements under the applicable accounting and regulatory framework.

The standard covers:

  • Cash and cash equivalents.
  • Operating activities.
  • Investing activities.
  • Financing activities.
  • Reporting cash flows.
  • Foreign currency cash flows.
  • Interest, dividends and income tax cash flows.

8.4 Classification of Cash Flows under AS-3

AS-3 (Revised) requires cash flows to be classified into three categories:

A. Cash Flows from Operating Activities

These arise from the principal revenue-producing activities of the enterprise.

B. Cash Flows from Investing Activities

These arise from the acquisition and disposal of long-term assets and investments other than cash equivalents.

C. Cash Flows from Financing Activities

These arise from activities that change the size and composition of the enterprise's capital and borrowings.

8.5 Important Provisions of AS-3 (Revised)

1. Reporting Cash Flows

A Cash Flow Statement must report cash flows during the period classified into operating, investing and financing activities.

2. Reporting Operating Activities

Cash flows from operating activities may be reported using the Direct Method or Indirect Method.

3. Interest and Dividends

Interest and dividends received and paid should be disclosed separately and classified consistently from period to period.

For non-financial enterprises, interest paid and dividends paid are generally classified as financing cash flows, while interest and dividends received are generally classified as investing cash flows.

For financial enterprises, interest paid and interest and dividends received are generally classified as operating cash flows. Dividends paid are generally financing cash flows.

4. Income Taxes

Cash flows arising from income taxes are generally classified as operating activities unless they can be specifically identified with investing or financing activities.

5. Foreign Currency Cash Flows

Cash flows arising from foreign currency transactions are recorded in the reporting currency using the exchange rate at the date of the cash flow, subject to the standard's provisions.

6. Non-Cash Transactions

Investing and financing transactions that do not require the use of cash or cash equivalents are excluded from the Cash Flow Statement. Such transactions should be disclosed separately where required.

Examples:

  • Acquisition of an asset by issuing shares.
  • Conversion of debt into equity.
  • Acquisition of assets through a lease without an initial cash payment.

7. Disclosure of Cash and Cash Equivalents

An enterprise should disclose the components of cash and cash equivalents and reconcile the amounts shown in the Cash Flow Statement with the corresponding items in the Balance Sheet.

8. Cash Flows on a Net Basis

Cash flows from certain activities may be reported on a net basis in the circumstances permitted by AS-3, such as certain receipts and payments made on behalf of customers or items with quick turnover, large amounts and short maturities.

8.6 Difference Between Direct and Indirect Methods

Basis Direct Method Indirect Method
Meaning Shows major classes of gross cash receipts and payments. Adjusts net profit for non-cash items, non-operating items and working capital changes.
Starting Point Cash receipts and payments. Net profit or loss before tax and extraordinary items, as applicable under the standard.

Preparation Requires information about cash receipts and payments. Uses profit figures and changes in balance sheet items.

8.7 Advantages of AS-3 (Revised)

  • Improves the quality of cash flow reporting.
  • Provides information about cash generation and utilisation.
  • Helps assess liquidity and financial flexibility.
  • Improves comparability of financial information.
  • Helps users evaluate future cash requirements.
  • Provides a structured classification of cash flows.

8.8 Limitations of Cash Flow Statement under AS-3

  • It does not measure net profit directly.
  • It excludes non-cash investing and financing transactions from cash flow totals.
  • It mainly reports historical cash movements.
  • It must be read along with other financial statements.
  • It does not fully explain the reasons for every change in financial performance.

8.9 Difference Between Cash Flow Statement and Funds Flow Statement

Basis Cash Flow Statement Funds Flow Statement
Meaning Shows movement of cash and cash equivalents. Traditionally shows changes in working capital.
Focus Cash inflows and outflows. Sources and applications of funds.
Basis Cash basis of reporting movements. Working capital movement basis in traditional analysis.
Objective To assess liquidity and cash management. To analyse changes in financial resources.
Standard AS-3 (Revised) deals with Cash Flow Statements. Traditional funds flow statement is an analytical tool.

11. IMPORTANT FORMULAS FOR EXAMINATION

Horizontal Change = Current Year - Previous Year
Percentage Change = (Change ÷ Previous Year) × 100
Vertical Percentage = (Individual Item ÷ Common Base) × 100
Trend Percentage = (Current Year Figure ÷ Base Year Figure) × 100
Current Ratio = Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities
Quick Ratio = Quick Assets ÷ Current Liabilities
Debt-Equity Ratio = Long-Term Debt ÷ Shareholders' Funds
Gross Profit Ratio = (Gross Profit ÷ Net Sales) × 100
Net Profit Ratio = (Net Profit ÷ Net Sales) × 100
Inventory Turnover Ratio = Cost of Goods Sold ÷ Average Inventory
Debtors Turnover Ratio = Net Credit Sales ÷ Average Trade Receivables
ROCE = (EBIT ÷ Average Capital Employed) × 100
Cash Flow = Cash Inflows - Cash Outflows

13. CONCLUSION

Financial Statement Analysis is an important technique for evaluating the financial health of a business organisation. It helps management, investors, creditors and other users understand profitability, liquidity, solvency and operational efficiency.

Horizontal Analysis compares financial information over different years, Vertical Analysis explains the composition of financial statements, and Trend Analysis identifies long-term financial movements. Ratio Analysis measures relationships between different financial figures and helps evaluate business performance.

Cash Flow Analysis provides information about the sources and uses of cash, while AS-3 (Revised) establishes the requirements for presenting cash flows under operating, investing and financing activities.

A proper understanding of these techniques enables students to analyse financial statements, solve practical accounting problems and make informed financial decisions.
