1. ACCOUNTING SYSTEMS AND TRANSACTION PROCESSING

1.1 Meaning of Accounting System

An accounting system is a systematic process used by a business organisation to identify, record, classify, summarise and communicate its financial transactions. It helps a business maintain proper financial records and understand its financial position and performance.

Every business performs several financial activities such as purchasing goods, selling products, paying salaries, receiving cash and making investments. An accounting system records these activities in an organised manner.

Definition: An accounting system is an organised arrangement of people, procedures, records and technology used to collect, process and communicate financial information of a business.

1.2 Objectives of an Accounting System

The main objectives of an accounting system are as follows:

  1. Recording Financial Transactions: To maintain a systematic record of all financial transactions of a business.
  2. Classification of Transactions: To classify transactions into different accounts such as cash, purchases, sales, assets and liabilities.
  3. Summarising Information: To prepare useful summaries of financial transactions through trial balances and financial statements.
  4. Determining Profit or Loss: To calculate the profit earned or loss suffered by the business during a particular period.
  5. Knowing Financial Position: To determine the assets, liabilities and owner's equity of a business.
  6. Decision-Making: To provide financial information to management for planning and making business decisions.
  7. Maintaining Control: To help prevent errors, fraud and misuse of business resources.

1.3 Components of an Accounting System

An accounting system consists of several important components:

Component Explanation
Source Documents Original documents such as invoices, receipts, bills and vouchers that provide evidence of transactions.
People Accountants, bookkeepers, managers and other employees who operate the accounting system.
Procedures Rules and methods followed for recording and processing financial transactions.
Accounting Records Books and registers such as journals, ledgers and cash books.
Technology Computers and accounting software used for processing financial information.
Internal Controls Measures used to protect assets and ensure the accuracy of accounting records.

1.4 Types of Accounting Systems

A. Manual Accounting System

A manual accounting system is a traditional system in which financial transactions are recorded and processed by hand using physical books and registers.

Features:

  • Transactions are recorded manually.
  • Journals and ledgers are maintained in physical books.
  • Calculations are performed by hand or basic calculators.
  • It requires more time and physical storage space.
  • It is suitable for businesses with a small number of transactions.

B. Computerized Accounting System

A computerized accounting system uses computers and accounting software to record, process, store and analyse financial information.

Features:

  • Transactions are recorded electronically.
  • Calculations and postings are performed automatically.
  • Financial reports can be generated quickly.
  • Data can be stored and retrieved easily.
  • It helps improve the speed and accuracy of accounting work.

1.5 Meaning of Transaction Processing

Transaction processing refers to the process of identifying, recording, classifying and processing financial transactions of a business. It converts the details of daily business activities into useful accounting information.

For example, when a business purchases goods for cash, the transaction is identified, supported by a purchase invoice or receipt and recorded in the appropriate accounting records.

1.6 Steps in Transaction Processing

Step Explanation
1. Identification Identify financial transactions that affect the business.
2. Collection of Documents Collect supporting documents such as invoices, bills and receipts.
3. Analysis Determine which accounts are affected and whether they should be debited or credited.
4. Recording Record the transaction in the journal or appropriate subsidiary book.
5. Classification Transfer transactions to their respective ledger accounts.
6. Summarisation Prepare a trial balance and summarise the accounting information.
7. Reporting Prepare financial statements to communicate financial results.
Example:
A business purchases furniture for ₹20,000 in cash.

The transaction affects two accounts:
  • Furniture Account increases by ₹20,000.
  • Cash Account decreases by ₹20,000.
The transaction is recorded in the accounting books and posted to the respective ledger accounts.

1.7 Importance of an Accounting System

  • Provides accurate financial records.
  • Helps determine business profitability.
  • Supports planning and budgeting.
  • Helps management control business activities.
  • Provides information to investors and creditors.
  • Assists in tax calculation and compliance.
  • Helps detect accounting errors and irregularities.

2. ACCOUNTING EQUATION

2.1 Meaning of Accounting Equation

The accounting equation is the basic foundation of the double-entry bookkeeping system. It represents the relationship between the assets, liabilities and owner's equity of a business.

Every business owns certain resources called assets. These resources are financed either by the owner or by outside parties such as banks and suppliers.

Assets = Liabilities + Owner's Equity

This equation shows that the total assets of a business are always equal to the total claims against those assets.

2.2 Components of Accounting Equation

A. Assets

Assets are the economic resources controlled by a business as a result of past events and from which future economic benefits are expected to flow to the business.

Examples of Assets:

  • Cash in hand
  • Cash at bank
  • Land and buildings
  • Furniture and machinery
  • Stock or inventory
  • Debtors or accounts receivable
  • Vehicles

B. Liabilities

Liabilities are the financial obligations or amounts payable by a business to outsiders. They arise from past transactions and are generally settled by paying cash, providing services or transferring other assets.

Examples of Liabilities:

  • Bank loans
  • Creditors or accounts payable
  • Outstanding salaries
  • Outstanding rent
  • Bills payable

C. Owner's Equity

Owner's equity represents the owner's financial interest in the assets of the business after deducting all liabilities. It is also known as capital or net worth.

It includes the capital introduced by the owner and accumulated profits, after considering drawings and losses.

Owner's Equity = Assets - Liabilities

2.3 Expanded Accounting Equation

The basic accounting equation can be expanded by including revenues, expenses and drawings.

Assets = Liabilities + Capital + Revenues
- Expenses - Drawings

This expanded equation explains how business income, expenses and withdrawals affect the owner's equity.

2.4 Importance of Accounting Equation

  1. It forms the foundation of double-entry bookkeeping.
  2. It helps understand the financial position of a business.
  3. It ensures that total debits and credits remain equal.
  4. It helps identify the effects of business transactions.
  5. It provides the basis for preparing financial statements.
  6. It helps maintain the accuracy of accounting records.

2.5 Numerical Example of Accounting Equation

Example:
A person starts a business with cash of ₹1,00,000.

Assets = Liabilities + Owner's Equity
₹1,00,000 = ₹0 + ₹1,00,000

Now the owner takes a bank loan of ₹50,000.
Total Assets = ₹1,50,000
Total Liabilities = ₹50,000
Owner's Equity = ₹1,00,000

Therefore:
₹1,50,000 = ₹50,000 + ₹1,00,000

The accounting equation remains balanced.

3. TRANSACTIONS AND THEIR EFFECTS ON ACCOUNTING EQUATION

3.1 Meaning of Business Transactions

A business transaction is a financial activity or event that can be measured in monetary terms and affects the financial position of a business.

Transactions may involve the purchase of goods, sale of products, payment of expenses, receipt of income, purchase of assets and borrowing of money.

3.2 Types of Business Transactions

A. Cash Transactions

Transactions in which payment or receipt takes place immediately in cash or through a bank are known as cash transactions.

Example: Goods purchased for cash ₹10,000.

B. Credit Transactions

Transactions in which payment is made or received at a later date are known as credit transactions.

Example: Goods purchased on credit from Mohan for ₹15,000.

C. Internal Transactions

Transactions or events that occur within the business and affect its accounting records are called internal transactions.

Example: Depreciation charged on machinery.

D. External Transactions

Transactions that take place between a business and an outside party are known as external transactions.

Example: Goods sold to a customer for ₹20,000.

3.3 Effects of Transactions on Accounting Equation

Every financial transaction affects at least two accounts or two aspects of the accounting equation. However, the equation always remains balanced.

Transaction Effect on Assets Effect on Liabilities Effect on Equity
Owner introduces cash ₹50,000 Increase No change Increase
Bank loan taken ₹20,000 Increase Increase No change
Furniture purchased for cash ₹10,000 One asset increases, another decreases No change No change
Goods purchased on credit ₹5,000 Increase Increase No change
Rent paid ₹2,000 Decrease No change Decrease
Commission received ₹3,000 Increase No change Increase
Owner withdraws cash ₹4,000 Decrease No change Decrease

3.4 Practical Illustration of Accounting Equation

Consider the following transactions of a newly established business:

Date Business Transaction Assets (₹) Liabilities (₹) Equity (₹)
1 Started business with cash ₹1,00,000 1,00,000 0 1,00,000
2 Purchased furniture for cash ₹20,000 1,00,000 0 1,00,000
3 Purchased goods on credit ₹15,000 1,15,000 15,000 1,00,000
4 Paid rent ₹5,000 1,10,000 15,000 95,000
5 Earned service revenue in cash ₹10,000 1,20,000 15,000 1,05,000
Verification:
Total Assets = ₹1,20,000
Total Liabilities = ₹15,000
Owner's Equity = ₹1,05,000

₹1,20,000 = ₹15,000 + ₹1,05,000
Thus, the accounting equation remains balanced.

3.5 Important Rules While Analysing Transactions

  • Every transaction has a dual effect.
  • An increase in assets may be accompanied by an increase in liabilities or equity.
  • Payment of expenses reduces assets and owner's equity.
  • Revenue increases owner's equity.
  • Drawings reduce owner's equity.
  • The accounting equation must always remain balanced.

4. CLASSIFICATION OF ACCOUNTS

4.1 Meaning of Account

An account is a summarised record of all transactions relating to a particular person, asset, liability, expense or income. It helps an organisation maintain a systematic record of its financial activities.

For example, all cash-related transactions are recorded in the Cash Account, while all salary-related transactions are recorded in the Salary Account.

4.2 Traditional Classification of Accounts

Under the traditional approach, accounts are classified into three main categories:

1. Personal Accounts
2. Real Accounts
3. Nominal Accounts

4.3 Personal Accounts

Personal accounts are accounts relating to individuals, firms, companies, banks and other organisations with whom the business has financial transactions.

Types of Personal Accounts:

A. Natural Personal Accounts

These accounts relate to human beings or individuals.

Examples: Ram's Account, Mohan's Account, Sita's Account.

B. Artificial Personal Accounts

These accounts relate to organisations or institutions recognised as legal persons.

Examples: ABC Ltd. Account, SBI Account, XYZ Company Account.

C. Representative Personal Accounts

These accounts represent a particular person or group of persons.

Examples: Outstanding Salary Account, Prepaid Rent Account, Accrued Interest Account.

Rule: Debit the Receiver
Credit the Giver
Example:
Goods sold to Ram for ₹10,000 on credit.

Ram receives the goods, so Ram's Account is debited.
Sales Account is credited.

Journal Entry:
Ram's A/c Dr. ₹10,000
    To Sales A/c ₹10,000

4.4 Real Accounts

Real accounts are accounts relating to the assets and properties owned or controlled by a business. These accounts generally continue from one accounting period to another through their closing balances.

Types of Real Accounts:

A. Tangible Real Accounts

These accounts relate to physical assets that can be seen and touched.

Examples: Cash Account, Furniture Account, Machinery Account, Building Account and Land Account.

B. Intangible Real Accounts

These accounts relate to assets that do not have physical existence but have economic value.

Examples: Goodwill Account, Patent Account, Copyright Account and Trademark Account.

Rule: Debit What Comes In
Credit What Goes Out
Example:
Furniture purchased for cash ₹15,000.

Furniture comes into the business, so Furniture Account is debited.
Cash goes out of the business, so Cash Account is credited.

Journal Entry:
Furniture A/c Dr. ₹15,000
    To Cash A/c ₹15,000

4.5 Nominal Accounts

Nominal accounts are accounts relating to expenses, losses, incomes and gains of a business. These accounts are generally closed at the end of the accounting period by transferring their balances to the Profit and Loss Account.

Examples of Nominal Accounts:

  • Salary Account
  • Rent Account
  • Wages Account
  • Commission Received Account
  • Interest Received Account
  • Discount Allowed Account
  • Depreciation Account
Rule: Debit All Expenses and Losses
Credit All Incomes and Gains
Example:
Salary paid to employees ₹8,000.

Salary is an expense, so Salary Account is debited.
Cash goes out of the business, so Cash Account is credited.

Journal Entry:
Salary A/c Dr. ₹8,000
    To Cash A/c ₹8,000

4.6 Difference Between Personal, Real and Nominal Accounts

Basis Personal Account Real Account Nominal Account
Meaning Related to persons and organisations. Related to assets and properties. Related to expenses, losses, incomes and gains.
Examples Ram, SBI, ABC Ltd. Cash, Furniture, Building. Salary, Rent, Commission.
Debit Rule Receiver What comes in Expenses and losses
Credit Rule Giver What goes out Incomes and gains
Remember: The traditional classification of accounts is based on Personal, Real and Nominal Accounts. Under the modern approach, accounts are commonly classified as Assets, Liabilities, Equity, Income and Expenses.

5. OWNER'S EQUITY, REVENUES AND EXPENSES

5.1 Meaning of Owner's Equity

Owner's equity is the amount of interest or claim that the owner has in the assets of a business after deducting all liabilities. It represents the owner's investment in the business together with accumulated profits, less drawings and losses.

Owner's Equity = Total Assets - Total Liabilities

5.2 Components of Owner's Equity

Owner's equity generally consists of the following elements:

A. Capital

Capital refers to the money or other assets introduced by the owner into the business for starting or operating the business.

Example: Mr. A starts a business with cash of ₹2,00,000. This amount is treated as capital.

B. Additional Capital

Additional capital refers to further investment made by the owner in the business after its establishment.

Example: The owner introduces another ₹50,000 into the business.

C. Profit

Profit is the excess of total revenue over total expenses during a particular accounting period. Profit increases owner's equity.

D. Drawings

Drawings refer to cash, goods or other assets withdrawn by the owner from the business for personal use. Drawings reduce owner's equity.

Example: The owner withdraws ₹5,000 cash from the business for personal expenses.

Closing Capital = Opening Capital
+ Additional Capital + Profit
- Drawings - Losses

5.3 Meaning of Revenue

Revenue refers to the income earned by a business through its normal business activities during a particular accounting period. It increases the owner's equity and contributes to the profitability of the business.

Examples of Revenue:

  • Sales Revenue
  • Service Revenue
  • Commission Received
  • Interest Received
  • Rent Received
  • Fees Received

5.4 Types of Revenue

A. Operating Revenue

Operating revenue is the income earned from the main business activities of an organisation.

Example: Sales of goods by a trading business or fees earned by a consultancy firm.

B. Non-Operating Revenue

Non-operating revenue is income earned from activities other than the main operations of a business.

Examples: Interest received on investments and rent received from property not used in the main business.

5.5 Meaning of Expenses

Expenses are the costs incurred by a business to earn revenue and carry out its normal business activities. Expenses reduce the profit of the business and decrease owner's equity.

Examples of Expenses:

  • Salary and wages
  • Office rent
  • Electricity expenses
  • Advertising expenses
  • Insurance expenses
  • Depreciation
  • Transportation expenses

5.6 Types of Expenses

A. Operating Expenses

Operating expenses are the expenses incurred in carrying out the regular business operations.

Examples: Salaries, rent, electricity, advertising and office expenses.

B. Non-Operating Expenses

Non-operating expenses are expenses that are not directly related to the main business operations.

Examples: Interest on loans and losses on the sale of fixed assets.

C. Direct Expenses

Direct expenses are costs that can be directly associated with the purchase or production of goods.

Examples: Direct wages, carriage inward and factory power.

D. Indirect Expenses

Indirect expenses are costs that cannot be directly associated with the production or purchase of a particular product.

Examples: Office salaries, advertising, office rent and administrative expenses.

5.7 Difference Between Revenue and Expenses

Basis Revenue Expenses
Meaning Income earned by the business. Costs incurred to earn revenue.
Effect on Profit Increases profit. Reduces profit.
Effect on Equity Increases owner's equity. Decreases owner's equity.
Examples Sales, commission and fees. Rent, salary and electricity.
Accounting Rule Normally credited. Normally debited.

5.8 Relationship Between Owner's Equity, Revenue and Expenses

Owner's equity, revenues and expenses are closely related to each other. Revenue increases owner's equity, whereas expenses decrease owner's equity. Drawings also decrease owner's equity.

Profit = Total Revenue - Total Expenses
Closing Equity = Opening Equity
+ Capital Introduced + Profit - Drawings
Numerical Example:

A business has the following financial information:
Opening Capital = ₹1,00,000
Additional Capital = ₹20,000
Revenue = ₹80,000
Expenses = ₹50,000
Drawings = ₹10,000

Step 1: Calculate Profit
Profit = Revenue - Expenses
= ₹80,000 - ₹50,000
= ₹30,000

Step 2: Calculate Closing Capital
Closing Capital = Opening Capital + Additional Capital + Profit - Drawings
= ₹1,00,000 + ₹20,000 + ₹30,000 - ₹10,000
= ₹1,40,000

Therefore, Closing Owner's Equity = ₹1,40,000.

9. CONCLUSION

Accounting systems and transaction processing form the foundation of financial accounting. The accounting equation explains the relationship between assets, liabilities and owner's equity. Every financial transaction affects the accounting equation, but the equation always remains balanced under the double-entry system.

The classification of accounts helps in recording transactions correctly, while the understanding of owner's equity, revenues and expenses helps in determining the profitability and financial position of a business. Therefore, these concepts are essential for understanding the complete accounting process.
