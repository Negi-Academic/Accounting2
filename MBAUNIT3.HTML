1. THE ACCOUNTING CYCLE

1.1 Meaning of Accounting Cycle

The accounting cycle refers to the complete process of identifying, recording, classifying, summarising and reporting the financial transactions of a business during an accounting period. It starts with the identification of business transactions and generally ends with the preparation of financial statements and closing of temporary accounts.

Every business performs different financial activities during the year. These activities are recorded systematically through the accounting cycle so that the business can determine its profit or loss and financial position.

Definition: The accounting cycle is a systematic series of accounting procedures followed by a business to record financial transactions and prepare financial statements for a particular accounting period.

1.2 Objectives of the Accounting Cycle

  1. Systematic Recording: To record all financial transactions in a proper and organised manner.
  2. Classification: To classify transactions into different accounts such as cash, sales, purchases, assets and liabilities.
  3. Accuracy: To maintain accuracy and consistency in accounting records.
  4. Determination of Profit or Loss: To calculate the financial result of business activities.
  5. Financial Position: To determine the assets, liabilities and owner's equity of a business.
  6. Financial Reporting: To prepare financial statements for management and other users.
  7. Decision-Making: To provide useful information for planning, controlling and making business decisions.

1.3 Steps Involved in the Accounting Cycle

The accounting cycle consists of the following major steps:

Step 1: Identification of Financial Transactions

The first step is to identify financial transactions that affect the business and can be measured in monetary terms.

Examples: Purchase of goods, sale of goods, payment of salary, receipt of cash and purchase of machinery.

Step 2: Preparation of Source Documents

Source documents are the original documents that provide evidence of business transactions. They are used as the basis for recording transactions in the accounting books.

Examples:

  • Cash memo
  • Purchase invoice
  • Sales invoice
  • Receipt
  • Debit note
  • Credit note
  • Bank statement
  • Payment voucher

Step 3: Recording Transactions in Journal

After identifying transactions and collecting source documents, the transactions are recorded in the Journal in chronological order. This process is called journalising.

Each journal entry contains a debit account, a credit account, the amount and a brief explanation called narration.

Step 4: Posting into Ledger

After recording transactions in the Journal, the entries are transferred to their respective Ledger Accounts. This process is known as posting.

The Ledger helps in collecting all transactions relating to a particular account at one place.

Step 5: Preparation of Trial Balance

A Trial Balance is prepared to check the arithmetical accuracy of ledger accounts. It contains the debit and credit balances of different ledger accounts.

Step 6: Passing Adjusting Entries

At the end of an accounting period, certain adjustments are made to ensure that income and expenses are recorded in the correct period.

Examples: Outstanding expenses, prepaid expenses, depreciation, accrued income and income received in advance.

Step 7: Preparation of Adjusted Trial Balance

After recording adjustment entries, an adjusted trial balance is prepared to show the updated balances of ledger accounts.

Step 8: Preparation of Financial Statements

The adjusted trial balance is used to prepare financial statements such as:

  • Trading Account
  • Profit and Loss Account
  • Balance Sheet
  • Cash Flow Statement, where applicable

Step 9: Closing Entries

At the end of the accounting period, temporary accounts such as revenue, expenses and drawings are closed or transferred to the appropriate accounts.

Step 10: Post-Closing Trial Balance

A post-closing trial balance is prepared after closing entries. It contains the balances of permanent accounts that are carried forward to the next accounting period.

1.5 Importance of the Accounting Cycle

  • Maintains systematic financial records.
  • Helps calculate business profit or loss.
  • Ensures proper classification of transactions.
  • Helps identify errors in accounting records.
  • Provides information for preparing financial statements.
  • Supports management in business planning.
  • Improves financial control and accountability.

1.6 Example of Accounting Cycle

Example:

Mr. Rahul starts a business with cash of ₹1,00,000. During the month, he purchases goods for ₹20,000, sells goods for ₹30,000, pays rent of ₹5,000 and pays salary of ₹3,000.

The accounting cycle will include:

  1. Identifying the above transactions.
  2. Collecting bills and receipts.
  3. Recording the transactions in the Journal.
  4. Posting the entries to Ledger Accounts.
  5. Preparing a Trial Balance.
  6. Making necessary adjustments.
  7. Preparing financial statements.

2. THE JOURNAL AND ITS SUB-DIVISIONS

2.1 Meaning of Journal

A Journal is the book of original entry in which business transactions are recorded for the first time in chronological order, that is, according to the date on which they occur.

It records the debit and credit aspects of every transaction according to the rules of double-entry bookkeeping.

Definition: A Journal is a book of original entry used to record financial transactions in chronological order with their corresponding debit and credit amounts.

2.2 Features of Journal

  1. It is the book of original entry.
  2. Transactions are recorded date-wise.
  3. Each transaction has a debit and a credit aspect.
  4. It contains a short explanation called narration.
  5. It provides the basis for posting transactions to the Ledger.
  6. It maintains a permanent record of business transactions.

2.7 Meaning of Sub-divisions of Journal

When the number of business transactions becomes large, recording every transaction in one Journal becomes difficult and time-consuming. Therefore, the Journal is divided into different specialised books known as subsidiary books or books of original entry.

Each subsidiary book records a particular type of transaction. This makes accounting work easier, faster and more systematic.

2.8 Types of Subsidiary Books

1. Purchase Book

A Purchase Book records only credit purchases of goods that are meant for resale or for use in production. Cash purchases and purchases of fixed assets are not recorded in this book.

Example: Goods purchased on credit from Mohan ₹20,000.

DateSupplierInvoice No.Amount (₹)
April 5Mohan10120,000

2. Sales Book

A Sales Book records only credit sales of goods that are purchased for resale. Cash sales and sales of fixed assets are not recorded in this book.

Example: Goods sold on credit to Ram ₹15,000.

DateCustomerInvoice No.Amount (₹)
April 8Ram20515,000

3. Purchase Returns Book

A Purchase Returns Book records goods returned to suppliers due to defects, poor quality, wrong specifications or other reasons. It is also known as the Returns Outward Book.

Example: Goods worth ₹2,000 returned to Mohan.

4. Sales Returns Book

A Sales Returns Book records goods returned by customers to the business. It is also known as the Returns Inward Book.

Example: Ram returned goods worth ₹1,500.

5. Cash Book

A Cash Book records all cash and bank receipts and payments of a business. It serves as both a book of original entry and a ledger account.

Types of Cash Book:

  • Single Column Cash Book: Contains only one amount column for cash transactions.
  • Double Column Cash Book: Contains cash and bank columns or cash and discount columns.
  • Triple Column Cash Book: Contains cash, bank and discount columns.
  • Petty Cash Book: Records small day-to-day expenses such as postage, stationery and local conveyance.

6. Bills Receivable Book

A Bills Receivable Book records all bills of exchange received by a business from its customers. These bills represent amounts receivable in the future.

7. Bills Payable Book

A Bills Payable Book records all bills of exchange accepted by a business in favour of its creditors. These bills represent amounts payable in the future.

8. Journal Proper

Journal Proper is used to record transactions that cannot conveniently be recorded in any of the other subsidiary books.

Examples:

  • Opening entries
  • Closing entries
  • Adjustment entries
  • Rectification entries
  • Depreciation entries
  • Transfer entries

2.9 Difference Between Journal and Subsidiary Books

Basis Journal Subsidiary Books
Meaning Primary book of original entry. Specialised books for particular transactions.
Recording Records different types of transactions. Records specific categories of transactions.
Use Suitable for smaller volumes of transactions. Useful when transactions are large in number.
Classification Transactions are recorded in chronological order. Transactions are classified according to their nature.

2.10 Advantages of Subsidiary Books

  • Reduces the burden of maintaining one Journal.
  • Saves time in recording transactions.
  • Allows division of accounting work among employees.
  • Helps in easy location of transactions.
  • Improves accuracy and efficiency.
  • Provides better control over different types of transactions.

3. THE LEDGER

3.1 Meaning of Ledger

A Ledger is the principal book of accounts in which all transactions recorded in the Journal or subsidiary books are classified and posted into individual accounts.

It contains separate accounts for assets, liabilities, capital, expenses and incomes. It helps a business know the total amount of each account at a particular time.

Definition: A Ledger is a book containing all the accounts of a business in which transactions are classified and recorded account-wise.

3.2 Features of Ledger

  1. It is the principal book of accounts.
  2. It contains separate accounts for different items.
  3. Transactions are posted from the Journal and subsidiary books.
  4. It helps determine the balance of each account.
  5. It provides the basis for preparing the Trial Balance.
  6. It helps prepare financial statements.

3.3 Objectives of Ledger

  • To classify transactions account-wise.
  • To determine the balance of each account.
  • To know the amount receivable from debtors.
  • To know the amount payable to creditors.
  • To provide information for preparing the Trial Balance.
  • To help determine the financial position of the business.

3.4 Format of Ledger Account

3.6 Posting in Ledger

Posting refers to the process of transferring entries from the Journal or subsidiary books to their respective Ledger Accounts.

Rules of Posting:

  1. The account debited in the Journal is posted on the debit side of that Ledger Account.
  2. The account credited in the Journal is posted on the credit side of that Ledger Account.
  3. The opposite account is mentioned in the Particulars column.
  4. The amount must be recorded correctly on both sides.

3.8 Balancing of Ledger Accounts

Balancing means calculating the difference between the total debit side and total credit side of an account. The difference is called the balance of that account.

Types of Ledger Balances

  • Debit Balance: When the total debit side is greater than the total credit side.
  • Credit Balance: When the total credit side is greater than the total debit side.
  • Nil Balance: When both sides are equal and there is no difference.

3.9 Advantages of Ledger

  • Provides a complete record of each account.
  • Helps determine balances easily.
  • Helps prepare the Trial Balance.
  • Provides information about debtors and creditors.
  • Helps in financial analysis.
  • Improves the organisation of accounting records.

3.10 Difference Between Journal and Ledger

4. THE TRIAL BALANCE

4.1 Meaning of Trial Balance

A Trial Balance is a statement that contains the debit and credit balances of all Ledger Accounts on a particular date. It is prepared to check the arithmetical accuracy of accounting records.

The total of the debit balances should be equal to the total of the credit balances when the double-entry system has been properly maintained.

Definition: A Trial Balance is a statement showing the debit and credit balances of various Ledger Accounts prepared on a particular date to test the arithmetical accuracy of accounting records.

4.2 Objectives of Trial Balance

  1. Checking Accuracy: To check the arithmetical accuracy of Ledger Accounts.
  2. Summarising Accounts: To present all ledger balances in one statement.
  3. Preparation of Financial Statements: To provide information for preparing Trading Account, Profit and Loss Account and Balance Sheet.
  4. Detection of Errors: To help identify certain accounting errors.
  5. Convenience: To make accounting information easier to understand and analyse.

4.3 Features of Trial Balance

  • It is a statement and not an account.
  • It is prepared on a particular date.
  • It contains debit and credit balances.
  • It is prepared from Ledger Accounts.
  • Total debit balances should equal total credit balances.
  • It is an important step in preparing financial statements.

4.5 Methods of Preparing Trial Balance

1. Total Method

Under this method, the total of the debit side and credit side of each Ledger Account is entered in the Trial Balance. The totals of both columns should agree.

2. Balance Method

Under this method, only the closing balances of Ledger Accounts are entered in the Trial Balance. Debit balances are placed in the debit column and credit balances are placed in the credit column.

3. Total and Balance Method

Under this method, both the total amounts and the closing balances of Ledger Accounts are shown in the Trial Balance.

4.6 Types of Trial Balance Errors

A Trial Balance may agree even when certain accounting errors exist. The following are the major types of errors:

A. Errors Disclosed by Trial Balance

  • Posting an amount to the wrong side of an account.
  • Posting an incorrect amount in one account.
  • Omitting a debit or credit posting from the Ledger.
  • Incorrect addition of Ledger Accounts.
  • Incorrect balancing of accounts.

B. Errors Not Disclosed by Trial Balance

  • Error of Omission: A transaction is completely omitted from the books.
  • Error of Commission: A transaction is recorded in the wrong account of the same category.
  • Error of Principle: A transaction is recorded against an accounting principle, such as treating machinery purchase as an expense.
  • Compensating Error: Two or more errors cancel out their effects.
  • Complete Reversal: The correct accounts are used, but the debit and credit aspects are reversed.

4.7 Limitations of Trial Balance

  1. It does not detect all types of accounting errors.
  2. It cannot detect complete omission of transactions.
  3. It cannot identify errors of principle.
  4. It cannot identify compensating errors.
  5. Agreement of Trial Balance does not guarantee complete accuracy of accounting records.
  6. It does not directly show the profit or loss of the business.

4.8 Difference Between Trial Balance and Balance Sheet

Basis Trial Balance Balance Sheet
Meaning Statement of ledger balances. Statement of financial position.
Objective Checks arithmetical accuracy. Shows assets, liabilities and equity.
Preparation Prepared before financial statements. Prepared as part of financial statements.
Accounts Contains balances of different ledger accounts. Contains assets, liabilities and equity.
Agreement Debit and credit totals should agree. Assets must equal liabilities plus equity.

5. COMPUTERIZED ACCOUNTING AND ITS IMPLICATIONS

5.1 Meaning of Computerized Accounting

Computerized accounting refers to the use of computers and accounting software for recording, processing, storing, analysing and reporting financial transactions of a business.

Instead of maintaining all accounting records manually, a business uses accounting software to perform various accounting activities quickly and accurately.

Definition: Computerized accounting is a system of maintaining financial records with the help of computers and accounting software to process and communicate accounting information.

5.2 Features of Computerized Accounting

  1. Automatic Recording: Transactions can be entered electronically and processed through the software.
  2. Speed: Large numbers of transactions can be processed in a short period.
  3. Accuracy: Automatic calculations reduce many common mathematical errors.
  4. Data Storage: Financial information can be stored electronically.
  5. Quick Reporting: Reports such as Trial Balance, Profit and Loss Account and Balance Sheet can be generated quickly.
  6. Data Retrieval: Old records can be searched and accessed easily.
  7. Integration: Accounting software may connect accounting with inventory, payroll and other business functions.
  8. Security: Passwords and access controls can help protect accounting information.

5.3 Objectives of Computerized Accounting

  • To reduce manual accounting work.
  • To improve the speed of accounting operations.
  • To maintain accurate financial records.
  • To generate financial reports quickly.
  • To reduce paperwork and storage requirements.
  • To improve financial control and monitoring.
  • To support better management decisions.

5.4 Components of Computerized Accounting System

Component Explanation
Hardware Physical devices such as computers, printers, scanners and servers.
Software Accounting applications used for recording and processing transactions.
Database Electronic storage of financial information.
Users Accountants, employees and managers who operate the system.
Procedures Rules and processes followed while entering and processing data.
Internal Controls Security measures used to protect information and prevent unauthorised access.

5.5 Examples of Accounting Software

Some commonly used accounting software and applications include:

  • TallyPrime: Used for bookkeeping, inventory, GST-related accounting and financial reporting.
  • BUSY Accounting Software: Used for accounting, inventory management and business reporting.
  • Zoho Books: Cloud-based accounting software used for invoicing, expenses and financial management.
  • QuickBooks: Used for bookkeeping, invoicing and financial reporting.
  • SAP: Enterprise software used for integrated financial and business management.

5.6 Working Process of Computerized Accounting

A computerized accounting system generally follows these steps:

  1. Data Entry: Financial transaction details are entered into the software.
  2. Transaction Processing: The software processes the information according to the selected accounts and rules.
  3. Automatic Posting: Transactions are reflected in the appropriate Ledger Accounts.
  4. Balance Calculation: Account balances are calculated automatically.
  5. Report Generation: The software prepares reports such as Trial Balance and financial statements.
  6. Data Backup: Financial data is backed up to reduce the risk of losing important records.

5.7 Advantages of Computerized Accounting

A. High Speed

Computerized accounting processes transactions much faster than manual accounting and saves valuable time.

B. Greater Accuracy

Automatic calculations reduce the possibility of mathematical errors, although incorrect data entry can still produce incorrect results.

C. Easy Preparation of Reports

Financial reports can be generated quickly without preparing every statement manually.

D. Better Data Storage

A large amount of financial information can be stored electronically with less physical storage space.

E. Easy Access to Information

Users can search and retrieve financial records whenever required, subject to their access permissions.

F. Better Financial Control

The system helps management monitor cash, expenses, sales and other financial activities.

G. Cost and Time Saving

Automation reduces repetitive work and may lower the cost of routine accounting activities.

5.8 Limitations of Computerized Accounting

  1. Initial Cost: Purchasing computers, software and other equipment may require a large initial investment.
  2. Technical Knowledge: Employees need proper training to operate the system.
  3. System Failure: Hardware or software problems may interrupt accounting operations.
  4. Data Security Risks: Financial information may be exposed to cyberattacks or unauthorised access.
  5. Data Loss: Information may be lost due to technical failure if proper backups are not maintained.
  6. Dependence on Electricity and Technology: The system may stop working during power or network failures.
  7. Maintenance Costs: Software updates, technical support and maintenance may involve additional expenses.

5.9 Difference Between Manual and Computerized Accounting

Basis Manual Accounting Computerized Accounting

Calculation Mostly performed manually. Mostly performed automatically.

Errors Manual calculation and posting errors may occur. Calculation errors are reduced, but data-entry and system errors remain possible.

5.10 Implications of Computerized Accounting

The introduction of computerized accounting has changed the way business organisations maintain their financial records. It has several important implications:

1. Improvement in Efficiency

Computerized systems reduce repetitive manual work and allow accountants to complete accounting activities more efficiently.

2. Better Decision-Making

Managers can obtain timely financial information and use it for planning, budgeting and controlling business activities.

3. Changes in the Role of Accountants

Accountants now spend more time analysing financial information, preparing reports and advising management rather than performing only routine calculations.

4. Better Financial Reporting

Computerized systems make it easier to prepare financial statements and other reports within a shorter period.

5. Improved Internal Control

User permissions, approval systems, audit trails and access restrictions help organisations monitor accounting activities.

6. Data Security and Privacy

Businesses must protect financial information through passwords, backups, encryption, security updates and proper access controls.

7. Integration of Business Activities

Modern accounting software can integrate accounting with inventory, payroll, sales and purchase management.

8. Professional Skill Development

Accountants need knowledge of accounting software, computers, data management and financial analysis to perform their duties effectively.

5.11 Precautions While Using Computerized Accounting

  • Use strong passwords and access controls.
  • Take regular backups of accounting data.
  • Install security updates and antivirus protection.
  • Allow access only to authorised users.
  • Verify transaction details before saving entries.
  • Maintain proper supporting documents.
  • Review financial reports regularly.
  • Maintain an audit trail of important changes.

9. CONCLUSION

The accounting cycle is an important part of financial accounting because it provides a systematic method of recording and reporting business transactions. The Journal records transactions for the first time, while the Ledger classifies them into separate accounts. The Trial Balance helps check the arithmetical accuracy of the accounting records and provides a basis for preparing financial statements.

Computerized accounting has made accounting work faster, easier and more efficient. It helps businesses maintain financial records, prepare reports, store information and make better decisions. However, proper training, data security and regular backups are necessary for the effective use of computerized accounting systems.
